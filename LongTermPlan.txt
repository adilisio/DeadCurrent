# DEAD CURRENT — Long-Term Game Plan

## 1. Vision

**DEAD CURRENT** is a first-person open-world action RPG set in a post-collapse Great Lakes archipelago.

The game should capture the qualities that make games like *Fallout 4: Far Harbor* compelling:

- dense exploration
- strong atmosphere
- environmental storytelling
- meaningful RPG progression
- firearms and melee combat
- character builds that affect how problems can be solved
- quests with multiple solutions
- factions with defensible but conflicting goals
- companions with opinions and personal stories
- player decisions that visibly alter the world

The goal is **not** to recreate Fallout.

The goal is to create an original RPG whose underlying design language feels immediately comfortable to someone who enjoys Bethesda-style exploration RPGs.

The project should favor **depth, density, and consequence over sheer map size**.

---

# 2. Core Fantasy

The player explores the remains of a once-connected Great Lakes region approximately sixty years after a cascading infrastructure disaster destroyed long-distance power, shipping, communications, and centralized government.

The world is defined by:

- abandoned shipyards
- grounded freighters
- flooded resorts
- isolated settlements
- marinas
- forests
- mines
- bunkers
- hydroelectric facilities
- military installations
- decaying industrial infrastructure

The signature environmental phenomenon is known as **the Current**.

The Current produces violent electromagnetic storms, unusual electrical activity, disrupted machinery, altered wildlife behavior, and intermittent transmissions from infrastructure that should have been dead for decades.

Some of those transmissions appear to contain voices.

Understanding what the Current is becomes one of the central mysteries of the game.

---

# 3. Narrative Premise

The player arrives aboard a trading vessel while searching for someone who disappeared while investigating one of the mysterious transmissions.

That personal investigation gradually reveals the existence of a massive pre-collapse emergency-management and infrastructure network buried throughout the region.

Several major groups want control of it.

Broadly:

- one faction wants to use it to restore regional civilization and trade
- one sees the transmissions as evidence that something new has emerged inside the abandoned network
- one controls surviving industrial infrastructure and believes restoring the network would give whoever controls it unacceptable power

No faction should exist simply to be the obviously correct choice.

The player should gradually learn enough about each group that major decisions become increasingly difficult rather than increasingly obvious.

---

# 4. Design Pillars

Every major feature should strengthen at least one of these pillars.

## Exploration

The world constantly rewards curiosity.

Approximately every 30–60 seconds of deliberate exploration should have the possibility of revealing something interesting:

- strange landmarks
- environmental stories
- hidden locations
- abandoned camps
- distress signals
- unusual NPC encounters
- wildlife behavior
- locked structures
- old terminals
- distant lights
- radio transmissions
- scavenging opportunities
- small mysteries

Exploration does not always lead to combat.

Discovery itself should be rewarding.

## RPG Agency

The player's build should change what they can do.

Skills, attributes, background, reputation, equipment, companions, prior discoveries, and quest decisions should regularly unlock different approaches.

A problem might be solved through:

- combat
- stealth
- persuasion
- intimidation
- engineering
- medicine
- exploration knowledge
- faction reputation
- finding a hidden route
- possession of a specific item
- prior quest decisions

## Consequence

The world remembers what the player does.

Choices should affect:

- quests
- NPC availability
- faction relationships
- dialogue
- merchants
- locations
- encounters
- companion opinions
- infrastructure
- local safety
- future quest opportunities

Consequences do not always need to be dramatic.

Small persistent changes make the world feel reactive.

## Atmosphere

The emotional target is:

**isolation + curiosity + danger + mystery**

The Great Lakes setting should feel beautiful, threatening, melancholy, and strange.

## Systemic Consistency

Different parts of the game should operate through shared underlying systems rather than one-off scripted solutions.

Quests, dialogue, terminals, NPCs, factions, combat, perks, world objects, and exploration should be able to interact through common state and condition systems.

---

# 5. World Structure

The final game should be a **dense regional open world**, not an enormous procedural continent.

Interesting locations are hand-authored.

Procedural tools may help place:

- vegetation
- debris
- rocks
- shoreline dressing
- background environmental detail

But the important content is intentionally designed.

That includes:

- towns
- dungeons
- quest spaces
- landmarks
- ambushes
- discoveries
- environmental stories
- faction locations

The rule is simple:

**Someone should have deliberately placed the interesting thing behind the hill.**

---

# 6. Core Gameplay Loop

The primary gameplay loop is:

**Explore → Discover → Investigate → Fight / Negotiate / Avoid → Loot → Learn → Decide → Change World → Improve Character → Explore**

The game should continuously move the player between:

- wilderness exploration
- interior locations
- combat
- dialogue
- scavenging
- character progression
- faction decisions
- environmental storytelling

No one subsystem should dominate the entire experience.

---

# 7. Combat

Combat combines firearms, melee weapons, enemies, environmental hazards, consumables, equipment, and character progression.

Quality matters more than quantity.

Weapons should feel distinct through:

- recoil
- handling
- sound
- animation
- impact
- damage behavior
- ammunition
- attachments
- situational strengths

Enemy behavior should support states such as:

- idle
- patrol
- suspicious
- investigate
- combat
- search
- flee
- return

Enemies should make believable tactical decisions rather than simply running directly toward the player.

---

# 8. Focus System

The Fallout-style tactical combat role is filled by an original mechanic called **Focus**.

Focus does not completely stop the world.

Instead, activating Focus temporarily sharpens the player's perception.

Possible effects include:

- heightened enemy readability
- easier tracking
- reduced sensory noise
- identification of vulnerable components
- contextual combat information

Character skills can improve what Focus reveals.

Examples might include:

- damaged respirator
- exposed battery
- cracked armor
- vulnerable leg
- weapon hand
- fuel container

The player still aims and fires manually.

Focus therefore reinforces character progression without replacing first-person combat.

---

# 9. Character Progression

The game uses an original:

**Attributes → Skills → Perks**

structure.

Skills should matter outside numerical damage bonuses.

They should affect:

- conversations
- Focus information
- crafting
- terminals
- environmental interactions
- medical treatment
- exploration
- quest solutions

Perks should create meaningful build identities rather than simply producing small percentage increases.

---

# 10. Inventory, Equipment, and Scavenging

The player scavenges physical objects throughout the world.

Loot should support:

- survival
- combat
- crafting
- trade
- exploration
- story discovery

The game should avoid becoming a constant junk-management simulator.

Items need clear reasons to exist.

Equipment eventually includes:

- firearms
- melee weapons
- ammunition
- armor
- medicine
- stimulants
- field compounds
- tools
- quest items
- salvage
- specialized exploration equipment

Heavy industrial or exploration rigs fill a role conceptually similar to heavy protective equipment without copying Fallout's power armor.

---

# 11. Crafting

Crafting should support player decisions rather than become a separate game.

Potential crafting categories include:

- weapon modifications
- ammunition
- medicine
- field supplies
- equipment repair
- exploration tools
- outpost improvements

The system should remain intentionally constrained.

Hundreds of recipes are not required.

---

# 12. Dialogue

Dialogue is a major gameplay system.

Conversation options can depend on:

- attributes
- skills
- reputation
- quest state
- discovered information
- inventory
- player background
- companion presence
- world state

Dialogue and quests use the same underlying condition system.

Dialogue outcomes can therefore directly modify the world.

---

# 13. Quest Architecture

Quests are data-driven structures composed of:

- stages
- objectives
- conditions
- events
- rewards
- consequences

Quest logic should not become enormous interconnected Blueprint graphs.

The same consequence system should support actions such as:

- advance quest stage
- grant or remove items
- change reputation
- change world state
- start combat
- unlock locations
- modify companion affinity

This makes quests easier to build and test as the game grows.

---

# 14. Factions and Reputation

Faction relationships are persistent.

Reputation should influence:

- dialogue
- quests
- prices
- hostility
- access
- information
- world encounters
- endings

The game should focus on a relatively small number of meaningful factions instead of dozens of shallow groups.

Faction conflict should grow organically from competing interests.

---

# 15. Companions

Companions are characters rather than equipment slots.

Each important companion should have:

- personal beliefs
- faction opinions
- affinity
- reactions to player choices
- personal dialogue
- a personal quest
- potential conflict with the player

Companions can also unlock unique knowledge or solutions.

---

# 16. World State

The game world must persist.

Important persistent state includes:

- quest progress
- NPC state
- inventory
- faction reputation
- containers
- doors
- destroyed objects
- activated machinery
- discovered locations
- companions
- world infrastructure
- outposts

Persistent actors should use stable identifiers.

Save/load is considered a foundational system, not a feature to bolt on at the end.

---

# 17. Outposts

The project may eventually contain a small-scale restoration/outpost system.

This is intentionally smaller than Fallout 4 settlement building.

The player may restore strategically important locations and infrastructure rather than construct entire towns piece by piece.

Possible examples:

- lighthouse
- relay station
- dock
- safehouse
- workshop
- power station

Outposts should strengthen exploration and world consequence rather than become a completely separate building game.

---

# 18. Art Direction

The visual target is **grounded stylization**, not maximum photorealism.

Important visual elements include:

- cold freshwater
- black rock
- oxidized ship hulls
- white birch
- dark pine
- faded maritime paint
- industrial lighting
- violent blue-white electrical storms
- decaying industrial structures

Strong silhouettes and consistent visual language matter more than asset complexity.

The environment needs a recognizable identity even when third-party assets are used.

---

# 19. Technology

Primary technology:

**Unreal Engine 5.8**

Development model:

**C++ + Blueprints + data-driven content**

Broad division of responsibility:

### C++

Use C++ for foundational systems:

- interaction framework
- inventory runtime
- combat infrastructure
- damage processing
- quests
- dialogue runtime
- save system
- persistent world state
- AI interfaces
- gameplay rules

### Blueprints / Data Assets

Use Blueprints and data for content creation:

- weapons
- NPCs
- encounters
- quests
- dialogue
- perks
- items
- locations
- effects

A designer should be able to create new content without modifying foundational engine code.

---

# 20. Shared Gameplay Language

Gameplay Tags should serve as a shared vocabulary across systems.

Examples:

Faction.DockUnion  
Damage.Electrical  
Status.Bleeding  
Skill.Engineering  
Quest.Main.Signal01  
WorldPower.Lighthouse

Systems should communicate through common state rather than tightly coupling themselves directly to one another.

---

# 21. RPG Systems Framework

Unreal's Gameplay Ability System is a strong candidate for systems involving:

- attributes
- damage
- temporary effects
- status conditions
- abilities
- perk effects

Potential shared concepts include:

- Health
- Stamina
- Focus
- Armor
- Exposure
- Bleeding
- Electrified
- Stimulated

The goal is to avoid creating unrelated frameworks for every type of gameplay effect.

---

# 22. Development Philosophy

Development follows:

**vertical-slice-first + systems-first**

We do not:

- build the entire map first
- create hundreds of weapons first
- build huge content libraries before proving the systems
- implement features simply because Fallout has them

The development ladder is:

### Phase 1 — Walking Skeleton
Spawn → move → interact → shoot → enemy dies → loot → save → load

### Phase 2 — Micro RPG
NPC → conversation → quest → combat → return → reward

### Phase 3 — Exploration Loop
Wilderness → POI → discovery → loot → story

### Phase 4 — RPG Layer
Attributes → skills → perks → dialogue checks

### Phase 5 — World State
Choices visibly alter locations and NPC behavior.

### Phase 6 — Vertical Slice
A polished 60–90 minute miniature of the complete game.

### Phase 7 — Production
Expand the world using proven systems.

### Phase 8 — Alpha
The complete game is playable from beginning to end.

### Phase 9 — Beta
Content is locked.

Focus becomes:

- optimization
- bugs
- balance
- accessibility
- usability
- polish

### Phase 10 — Release
Final QA, packaging, distribution, and launch.

---

# 23. Vertical Slice Vision

The vertical slice begins during an electrical storm.

The player reaches a small settlement whose lighthouse is malfunctioning.

The player can:

- meet residents
- trade
- receive quests
- explore wilderness
- fight enemies
- discover environmental stories
- enter a dungeon beneath the lighthouse
- investigate why the lighthouse failed

Eventually the player discovers that the apparent malfunction may have been intentional.

Possible resolutions include:

- restore the lighthouse
- redirect its power
- sabotage it
- expose what actually happened

The outcome changes elements such as:

- nighttime safety
- NPC survival
- faction reputation
- merchant inventory
- dialogue
- future encounters

This slice should demonstrate the entire intended game in miniature.

---

# 24. Initial Production Scope

Before attempting a massive RPG, target approximately:

- 1–2 km² of dense terrain
- one settlement
- three substantial wilderness locations
- two dungeons
- two factions
- one companion
- roughly five enemy archetypes
- three firearm families
- one melee family
- one armor system
- approximately six substantial quests
- approximately fifteen smaller encounters

This is enough to prove whether the game works.

Expansion happens only after these systems are stable and enjoyable.

---

# 25. Scope Protection Rule

A feature is not added because another RPG has it.

A feature is added because DEAD CURRENT needs it.

Features that should not become early priorities include:

- enormous settlement construction
- hundreds of perks
- hundreds of NPC schedules
- radiant quest systems
- dozens of weapon modification slots
- hundreds of crafting recipes
- full economic simulation
- elaborate limb simulation
- massive crowds

The project needs the **illusion of a larger simulation**, not necessarily the cost of actually simulating everything.

---

# 26. Long-Term Success Condition

DEAD CURRENT succeeds when a player finishes the game remembering:

- places they discovered
- people they cared about
- strange things they found
- decisions they struggled with
- consequences they did not expect
- character builds that opened different possibilities
- the feeling of traveling alone through a beautiful and dangerous Great Lakes wilderness

The game should make players wonder:

**"What's over there?"**

And regularly reward them for finding out.
